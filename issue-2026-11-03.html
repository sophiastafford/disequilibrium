Keeping up with AI & the Entry-Level Job Market | November 3, 2026

What Changed This Week

Who reads your résumé first? In tech, 83% of the time, the answer is a machine. AI is now the first reader of your application for jobs that AI is also making scarcer.

That double role changes what a rejection means. A Northeastern University survey of 8,174 people who make hiring decisions found that 54% use AI to screen résumés, and that among those whose decisions AI has influenced, 26% are hiring fewer entry-level candidates. The same technology is thinning the openings and sorting the applicants, and no one has asked for a human to check the second half. Medicine shows what that norm looks like when it exists. Only 3% of Americans will accept an AI making a diagnosis alone, while 47% accept it with a doctor reviewing the result.

The week’s other half is where the work went. Banks and hospital systems now outbid technology companies for software engineers, and 28% of those same hiring managers expect entry-level staff to take on higher-level work rather than less. The rest of this issue follows both sides of that door and the parts of it you can still reach.

Tech

A Northeastern University survey of 8,174 hiring decision-makers found that 54% screen résumés with AI, and 83% do in tech. About half said AI had influenced their decisions. Within that group, 26% are hiring fewer entry-level candidates and 7% are hiring more, while 28% expect entry-level staff to produce more or take on higher-level work. Almost half said AI experience is not an explicit hiring criterion, and about 40% treat AI use on a writing or coding assignment as a plus against 21% who say it would discourage an offer. For students applying into tech, saying how you used AI on a take-home assignment is roughly twice as likely to help as to hurt. (Northeastern Global News)

Banks pay about 13% more and health care and life sciences firms up to 17% more than technology companies for software engineers. Randstad, a recruiting company, reported the gap on October 29. Its chief executive described the AI investment wave moving out of the technology sector and into ordinary businesses that are building their own technical teams. The comparison covers software engineers as a whole rather than entry-level hires. For computer science majors who have only been targeting large technology employers, the openings now sit with hospital systems, insurers and banks, which run different recruiting calendars and appear at different career fairs. (CNBC | Quartz)

Amazon cut about 250 mostly software roles in its devices and services unit a day after publishing an internal memo telling engineers that AI usage is not productivity. The September memo asked software development engineers to apply AI to real customer problems rather than chase usage numbers, and it warned that token consumption, adoption rates and volume of code changes do not necessarily reflect productivity and can add cost and complexity. Amazon attributed the cuts to a review of its structure and product roadmap, not to AI. For engineering students, the memo is the more useful document, because a major employer is stating in writing how it intends to judge AI-assisted work. (Business Today | The Federal)

Law

On October 28, Anthropic and Everlaw both shipped tools that automate the tasks a first-year associate is hired to do. Anthropic added legal Artifacts, editable prepackaged workflows covering cite checking, jurisdiction survey matrices, regulatory horizon scans and tabular contract review, with versions built by Goodwin, PwC, Foley & Lardner and Harvey. Everlaw, an electronic discovery company, introduced Omni, an agentic AI suite, and Workflows, which automates document review for complex litigation. Document review and cite checking are the billable volume that justified large first-year classes. For law students, the question worth asking at an interview is no longer whether a firm uses AI but what work it still expects a first-year to produce unassisted. (Artificial Lawyer | eDiscovery Today | Law.com)

Harvey bought Aida on October 29, its fifth acquisition of 2026. Aida was founded by Glean co-founder Piyush Prahladka and Atish Das Sarma, and its search and machine learning engineers join Harvey’s product and engineering organization. Harvey says the work will let each firm build its own ontology so that AI agents have more firm-specific context. Harvey also bought Guardails in September and Benchmark in July, and the price was not disclosed. For law students, legal AI companies now compete with firms for the same graduates, and a product role at one is worth treating as a branch of a legal career rather than an exit from it. (Artificial Lawyer)

Healthcare

Only 3% of Americans will accept an AI making a diagnosis on its own, while 47% accept it with a human reviewing the result. The Pew Research Center surveyed 3,488 adults and found the same pattern across clinical tasks. For analyzing medical scans, 6% accept AI working alone against 60% with review. Sixty percent said they would be uncomfortable with their own doctor relying on AI for diagnosis and treatment, and adults aged 18 to 29 have grown more pessimistic, with 45% now expecting worse patient outcomes against 29% in 2022. For pre-med and nursing students, the public is drawing its line at human sign-off rather than at AI involvement, which suggests the clinical roles you are training for get rebuilt around verification rather than removed. (Pew Research Center)
